# Editorial decisions

Use these as contextual judgments, not universal prohibitions. The workflow and diagnostic questions are original design choices; consult `sources.md` for the traditions that inform them.

## Resolve a rule through its purpose

| Common advice | Intended benefit | Keep the apparent exception when… | Check the actual result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prefer active voice | Make relevant agency visible. | The actor is unknown, irrelevant, or already established; the object is the continuing topic. | Did the edit invent an actor or break topic continuity? |
| Remove qualifiers | Remove empty hesitation and intensifiers. | The qualifier defines uncertainty, degree, population, conditions, or evidential strength. | Did “may,” “sometimes,” or “after adjustment” disappear? |
| Prefer short sentences | Reduce parsing load. | A longer sentence holds a comparison, dependency, qualification, or rhythmic movement together. | Can the reader attach each clause without rereading? |
| Use concrete language | Give the reader a graspable claim or image. | An abstraction is the intended scientific construct, category, or level of generality. | Did a vivid rewrite introduce a detail that the source never supplied? |
| Avoid repetition | Remove needless duplication. | Repetition stabilizes a technical referent, sustains emphasis, or creates a pattern. | Does variation imply a new entity or erase a purposeful recurrence? |
| Prefer verbs to nominalizations | Expose an action and its participants. | A noun names a measured construct, established method, object of comparison, or paragraph topic. | Did “prediction,” “reliability,” or “randomization” lose its technical role? |
| State positively | Make the intended assertion direct. | Negation expresses the precise finding, boundary, refusal, contrast, or irony. | Are “not demonstrated” and “absent” being confused? |
| Lead with the point | Orient a reader who needs a decision or explanation. | The form depends on discovery, narrative timing, persuasion through evidence, or an unanswered question. | Is the reader oriented even if the destination is delayed? |
| Use plain words | Remove gratuitous difficulty. | The less common word is precise, economical for this audience, or expressive in a way the plain substitute is not. | Does the replacement require more explanation or change the sense? |
| Apply a punctuation convention | Make syntax and treatment consistent. | Quotation integrity, a required house style, code syntax, or an intentional literary effect takes priority. | Is the mark wrong, inconsistent with the chosen convention, or merely unfashionable? |

## Build paragraphs that do work

For a difficult paragraph, use a short working outline: **question → claim → support → qualification → consequence**. Select the parts actually needed; vary the sequence when the reasoning calls for it. Use this as a diagnostic aid, not a paragraph template.

Check four relationships:

1. **Entry:** Give the reader a recognizable topic or a clear connection to what preceded it. Avoid opening with an unexplained pronoun, acronym, or abstract verdict.
2. **Movement:** Make each sentence develop, test, exemplify, qualify, or draw a consequence from the preceding material. Consecutive true statements may still fail to form an argument.
3. **Attachment:** Keep a qualification within the scope of its claim. Reordering can turn a local exception into a disclaimer about the whole paragraph, or the reverse.
4. **Landing:** End where the paragraph has earned emphasis. Avoid adding a stock recap that merely repeats the opening.

Repair an inferred logical gap by recovering support already present, narrowing the conclusion, or identifying the missing premise. Do not insert “therefore” to make adjacent claims look deductively connected. Transitions must express a relationship that the evidence supports.

## Control the reader's effort

Distinguish a demanding idea from an unnecessarily demanding sentence. Define a term before relying on it; place the main verb where its subject remains easy to retrieve; unpack noun clusters when their relationships are uncertain. Keep a necessary technical label intact, then explain it.

Expansion can be the economical choice. A short definition may save repeated inference throughout a document. Conversely, a familiar concept need not receive an elementary explanation in every paragraph. Infer the audience's knowledge from the task and supplied material.

Use sentence variation to express relationships. A short sentence can establish a claim. A longer sentence can compare alternatives or hold a qualification beside the claim it limits. Repeatedly splitting at every conjunction can destroy those relationships.

## Preserve expressive control

Identify what a striking phrase does before altering it. Productive ambiguity can invite reflection; accidental ambiguity can obscure the actor in a procedure. Treat them differently.

Prefer a single well-chosen image to added decorative metaphors. Keep humour's timing and leave implied conclusions implicit when explanation would kill the effect. Do not replace an author's restrained sentence with generic enthusiasm.

For a close editorial choice, consider the source alongside the candidate. Choose the candidate only when its benefit is clear in context. Do not require an exact percentage of changed words, a fixed sentence-length distribution, or an invented “voice score.”

## Distinguish kinds of intervention

Use these labels only when a report is requested or a consequential change needs disclosure:

- **Correction:** Repair a demonstrable error, citing the governing source when disputed.
- **House-style choice:** Apply an agreed convention among legitimate alternatives.
- **Editorial suggestion:** Improve readability or effect through judgment; explain the benefit without pretending that a rule demands it.
- **Substantive change:** Alter a claim, implication, recommendation, or argument. Ground it in the brief or evidence and identify it explicitly.

Do not treat a plausible explanation of an edit as proof of its value. For a substantial change, identify the problem in the source before adopting the replacement, then check that the replacement actually solves it.
