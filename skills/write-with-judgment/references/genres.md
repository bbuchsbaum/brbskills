# Other genres

Scientific prose is covered in [scientific.md](scientific.md); documentation, procedures, and instructions in [technical.md](technical.md). Select the relevant section below. Infer genre from purpose and content, not from a filename alone. Preserve a required publication style, then use these defaults as editorial judgment.

## Explanations, essays, and arguments

Establish the question and what the reader needs to understand. Choose examples that clarify a mechanism or distinction. Make the difference between evidence, interpretation, analogy, and speculation visible.

Give a fair account of a position before criticizing it. Make objections specific enough to answer. Preserve uncertainty about motives and causes; a plausible story is still an inference. Do not replace an argument with an unsupported contrast formula or a series of confident summaries.

Let paragraphs accumulate understanding. Define only the terms the reader needs; return to examples when they make the reasoning easier to assess. Keep useful caveats near their claims. Remove repeated announcements of what the next paragraph will do when the progression is already clear.

## Professional letters, reports, and concise messages

Lead with the reason for writing, requested decision, or useful result. Give enough context to make the action reasonable. Keep dates, names, commitments, responsibility, and politeness intact.

Do not turn a request into an order to save words, or a tentative commitment into a promise. Preserve the social force of the wording: gratitude, disagreement, condolence, invitation, or refusal. A sentence can be efficient without being abrupt.

Avoid unsupported impact claims and generic enthusiasm. Prefer a concrete outcome and its limitation. If the brief lacks facts, leave a visible gap or make the draft appropriately general; do not invent achievements, recipients, deadlines, or promises.

## Literary work, including poetry

In verse, line breaks, metre, sound, and white space are content; do not reflow or regularize them unless asked. Protect imagery, syntax, implication, tone, point of view, and timing where they contribute to the intended effect. Allow fragments, repetition, unusual diction, long sentences, and indirectness when the writing earns them.

Distinguish errors from a narrator's or character's language. Do not standardize dialogue merely because it departs from formal expository English. Do not explain an image's meaning unless asked. Keep intentional uncertainty while repairing accidental confusion about who speaks or what happens.

For light editing, preserve strong lines. For an authorized transformation, preserve the requested emotional or artistic aim rather than every feature of the old surface. In fresh fictional work, invented detail is part of the brief; in factual literary nonfiction, it still requires support.

## Summaries and adaptations

Use the requested audience, length, and purpose to select what survives. Rank the central finding or decision, the basis for it, and the limitation that could change its interpretation. Cut secondary examples before decisive qualifications.

Preserve disagreement between sources. Avoid merging differently scoped findings into a single universal result. Track who holds each view. Condensation may omit content; it must not make the retained content imply the opposite or more than the original supports.

If the length requirement and necessary coverage conflict, use the clearest achievable summary and state the concrete conflict briefly. Do not solve it by deleting negation, uncertainty, or the population to which the result applies.
