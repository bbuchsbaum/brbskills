# Style: the complete 21-reminder guide

Use this reference when a draft or revision needs judgment about voice, rhythm, restraint, dialogue, or clarity. Improve the work within its effective style. Do not make every author sound alike.

Source: *The Elements of Style*, fourth edition, Chapter V, supplied PDF pp. 62–74. Page numbers identify 1-based PDF positions, not printed pagination. The source presents these as flexible reminders from a writer's experience, not an infallible system. Examples are compressed descriptions or short source phrases; **Adaptation** labels distinguish this skill's practical qualifications from the book's advice.

## Protect style from mechanical improvement

**Source: PDF pp. 62–64.**

- Correct grammar and explicit meaning do not guarantee effective prose. The source contrasts Thomas Paine's eight-word sentence with grammatically valid rearrangements, including “Soulwise, these are trying times”; the force depends on arrangement and cadence.
- A Thomas Wolfe inversion loses emotional and sensuous force when rewritten in ordinary subject–verb order. Do not “repair” an effective inversion solely because a conventional order is simpler.
- Faulkner's long, flowing treatment of convalescent languor and Hemingway's clipped sequence about Manuel are both distinctive. Frost's stillness and Whitman's bodily exuberance likewise differ without either being an error. **Operational lesson:** preserve effective sentence movement, compression, repetition, vocabulary, and distance from the subject. Do not normalize all writers into one plain style.
- Style is not decorative sauce added afterward. The source commends plainness, order, sincerity, patience, and thoughtful revision while recognizing that individual voice emerges through the writing.


## Reminders 1 through 5

### 1. Place yourself in the background

**Source: PDF p. 64.** Direct attention to the subject and its substance; avoid performative personality that displaces the work. Honest, competent writing reveals the writer without posing. Composition also develops thought. **Adaptation:** not a ban on first person, opinion, memoir, or an explicitly personal voice.

### 2. Write in a way that comes naturally

**Source: PDF pp. 64–65.** Start with words and phrases available to your own ear; naturalness is no guarantee against error. Read and admire good work; imitation can nourish style without deliberate mimicry. **Adaptation for LLMs:** preserve the user's effective voice and don't simulate a famous author's manner from a few superficial markers.

### 3. Work from a suitable design

**Source: PDF p. 65.** Match planning to scale: laundry list can follow the pile; biography needs an organizing scheme. A tent and cathedral require different designs. Exception: an impassioned letter may suffer if emotions are forced into rigid order; even adventurous writing can have a guiding direction. Avoid one universal essay template.

### 4. Write with nouns and verbs

**Source: PDF p. 65.** Carry information in accurate nouns and verbs; modifiers cannot rescue an inaccurate headword. **Explicit exception:** adjectives and adverbs are indispensable; “airy mountain” and “rushy glen” supply the poem's force. Do not count or indiscriminately delete modifiers.

### 5. Revise and rewrite

**Source: PDF pp. 65–66.** Revision can require moving whole passages, not merely polishing words. Preserve original and revision so experiments are reversible; major rearrangement is normal. Review on paper or screen as useful. **LLM adaptation:** compare the revision against the original for unsupported additions, lost qualifications, and damaged voice.

## Reminders 6 through 10

### 6. Do not overwrite

**Source: PDF p. 66.** Remove ornamental excess and passages sustained by the pleasure of producing prose. Source allows that exceptional rich writing can justify itself; the objection is excess without sufficient substance or vigor. Reread after drafting; don't confuse ornate with automatically bad or short with automatically good.

### 7. Do not overstate

**Source: PDF p. 66.** Unsupported exaggeration or a casual superlative undermines trust in nearby claims as well as the claim itself. **Adaptation:** preserve evidence strength, limits, and scope; *strengthen the prose* does not authorize strengthening the evidence.

### 8. Avoid the use of qualifiers

**Source: PDF p. 66.** Source targets vague weakening intensifiers—*rather, very, little, pretty*—and satirically overuses them to show the effect. **Adaptation:** distinguish verbal tics from epistemic and quantitative qualifications. Keep *may, in this sample, approximately, under these conditions* when they constrain a claim.

### 9. Do not affect a breezy manner

**Source: PDF pp. 66–67.** The alumni-news example uses slang, exaggerated familiarity, typographic play, and a self-important preamble without news; the improved version immediately reports a former rower's move from sales management to gondolier. Substance and reporting create interest better than forced chumminess. **Adaptation:** warmth and informality can remain when genuine and requested.

### 10. Use orthodox spelling

**Source: PDF pp. 67–68.** Avoid *nite/thru/pleez* in ordinary prose: unfamiliar forms make readers decode the surface. The source acknowledges spelling changes by agreement and gives *thruway* as a context where compression suits road signs. Preserve proper names, quotations, code, dialect, established variants, and purposeful design.

## Reminders 11 through 15

### 11. Do not explain too much

**Source: PDF p. 68.** Let dialogue reveal the speaker's state instead of burdening every tag with *consolingly/grumblingly* or showy substitutes such as *consoled/congratulated*. *Said* needs no ornamental rescue. **Adaptation:** retain necessary explanation for a reader who lacks context; do not delete useful teaching or disambiguation merely for brevity.

### 12. Do not construct awkward adverbs

**Source: PDF pp. 68–69.** Avoid ungainly manufacture: climbed *tiredly* → *wearily*; cord lay *tangledly* → *in tangles*; *thusly* → *thus*, *muchly* → *much*. Source also prefers *over* to *overly*. **Adaptation:** current natural use and meaning matter; don't treat all -ly forms or the word *tiredly* as forbidden.

### 13. Make sure the reader knows who is speaking

**Source: PDF p. 69.** Supply enough attribution for long dialogue and put tags at natural pauses: “Now, my boy, we shall see,” he said, “how…” → “Now, my boy,” he said, “we shall see how…”; “they would never,” she added, “consent…” → put the tag after “What's more.” Preserve speech continuity; read/hear the rhythm.

### 14. Avoid fancy words

**Source: PDF pp. 69–70.** Prefer plain words when equally apt, but context and sound outrank price tags or etymology. *Gut* is livelier than *intestine* yet can be too coarse; *tummy* can be coy. Lincoln's “Four score and seven” has a timing/cadence benefit that “eighty-seven” would lose. **Explicit exceptions:** final prepositions can sound stronger and more natural; “the worst tennis player is me” can show better judgment than formal “is I”; “to really like” may improve on an unsplit infinitive. Omit *that* from “She knew she could…” but retain it in “He felt that his big nose…” to prevent a misleading initial parse. Do not apply brevity mechanically.

### 15. Do not use dialect unless your ear is good

**Source: PDF pp. 70–71.** Represent a variety accurately, economically, and consistently; excessive phonetic display tires readers. Source contrasts spellings *oncet/wunst* and insists on consistency. **Adaptation:** preserve authentic dialect rather than “correcting” identity; do not fabricate accents or caricature speakers from demographics.

## Reminders 16 through 21

### 16. Be clear

**Source: PDF p. 71.** Clarity is a virtue without being the only artistic goal; intentional obscurity or wild language should still serve an intelligible purpose. When syntax is hopelessly tangled, rebuild or split the sentence rather than patching indefinitely. Road signs, intimate letters, and travel messages illustrate consequential ambiguity. Check what the sentence actually communicates, not what the writer knows privately.

### 17. Do not inject opinion

**Source: PDF p. 71.** Keep opinion relevant to the task and moment. A polite refusal to speak at a cat hospital need not become a denunciation of cats; explain the decision as needed. **Adaptation:** don't neutralize an essay's argument or omit requested judgment; avoid gratuitous commentary and patronizing asides.

### 18. Use figures of speech sparingly

**Source: PDF pp. 71–72.** Allow readers time to absorb an image; a rapid succession of comparisons distracts. Preserve a metaphor's internal logic—don't start with a swordfish and finish with an hourglass. Use figures that clarify, evoke, or organize; avoid both decorative clutter and a blanket metaphor ban.

### 19. Do not take shortcuts at the cost of clarity

**Source: PDF p. 72.** Spell out unfamiliar organization names before initials (source example: Mothers Against Drunk Driving → MADD). A shortcut that makes readers reconstruct missing context wastes their time. **Adaptation:** define relevant technical acronyms for the actual audience; don't expand universally familiar units/initialisms reflexively or use length alone as the metric.

### 20. Avoid foreign languages

**Source: PDF p. 72.** Don't scatter foreign phrases merely to display learning or disregard reader comfort. Source permits convenient/necessary borrowing. **Adaptation:** preserve precise borrowed terms, proper names, quotations, and culturally meaningful wording; explain when the audience needs it.

### 21. Prefer the standard to the offbeat

**Source: PDF pp. 72–74.** New slang, advertising diction, business jargon, officialese, and critical praise can advertise attitude without conveying thought. Source examples include *obsoletes/accessorized*, business *updated/finalize*, and critical *luminous/taut*. Inspect the actual meaning: does *finalize* mean finish or put into final form? **Explicit exceptions:** language changes; new words can survive and become useful; *update* can be apt; no word should be dismissed offhand; no idiom or accent is taboo; a real innovator should innovate. **Adaptation:** retain established technical terms and contemporary ordinary words. Don't freeze language at the book's publication date.

## Closing attitudes

**Source: PDF p. 74.**

- Style emerges from attitudes and judgment as well as technique. Trust the reader's intelligence; avoid patronizing explanation.
- Be sympathetic to the reader's effort while maintaining integrity rather than following fashion. The book's literary “audience of one” stance is **not** an instruction for an assistant to disregard the user's needs or brief.
- The Stevenson cow example celebrates a memorable, exact arrangement of plain language; no checklist guarantees that effect. Preserve felicity when already present.
