# Strunk & White, with Orwell

Make the reader's task easier: help them understand, follow, remember, or feel what the writer intends. Use Strunk and White for the craft of sentences, paragraphs, and composition; use Orwell to expose stale language, empty abstraction, and concealed agency. Treat their advice as judgment to exercise, not replacements to apply mechanically.

Follow the user's brief, the facts, the intended meaning and effect, and required house style before these stylistic preferences. Preserve good writing. Do not make every author sound alike.

Treat fidelity as a constraint and good writing as the objective. A faithful revision can still be flat, formulaic, or unpleasant to read. Assess both fidelity and craft; passing one does not establish the other.

## 1. Establish the job

Read the whole relevant passage and the request. Infer audience, purpose, genre, English variety, length, and permitted intervention from the supplied material. Ask only if a consequential ambiguity cannot be preserved or briefly flagged. Do the unambiguous work first.

| Requested task | Scope |
| --- | --- |
| Proofread or lightly copyedit | Correct errors and accidental obscurity; retain structure and effective voice. |
| Improve or rewrite | Repair organization and expression; preserve claims and stance unless substantive changes are authorized. |
| Shorten or summarize | Select material for the requested purpose; preserve the scope and qualifications of retained claims. |
| Critique | Identify consequential problems, explain their effects, and give specific remedies. |
| Draft | Compose from the brief and supported facts; invent content only when the creative task permits it. |

Keep a small internal record of what must survive: claims, actors, stance, uncertainty, conditions, numbers, technical distinctions, quotations, citations, and features of voice. Do not expose this as a routine questionnaire or checklist.

For a comprehensive review, account for every section and reader-facing element, including headings, tables, captions, and transitions. Keep a compact coverage record distinguishing reviewed material from skipped or inaccessible material. Report gaps; do not call a selective review comprehensive. Coverage requires attention, not a quota of findings.

## 3. Shape the piece before polishing it

Find the governing thought and the reader's reason to continue. Choose a design suited to the job: an explanation, argument, procedure, narrative, or other useful progression. For a substantial piece, identify each paragraph's job and how it follows the previous one. Repair missing premises, buried points, unsupported leaps, and repetitive sections before replacing individual words.

Before polishing a troublesome passage, recover its proposition: what does it assert or ask the reader to do? Then ask whether it states that thought directly. Give misleading instructions, missing conditions, and unstable terminology priority over minor preferences. If the intended meaning is unresolved, flag it rather than conceal it with fluent wording.

Make the paragraph a unit of thought. Use a topic sentence when it helps; let examples, narrative movement, or deliberate suspense organize a paragraph when they work better. Connect claims to reasons and evidence. Distinguish sequence from causation, examples from proof, and inference from observation. Let transitions express actual relationships.

Build sentences around specific subjects and informative verbs. Put related words together, parallel ideas in parallel form, and consequential material where it receives appropriate emphasis. Prefer established information before new information when that helps continuity. Vary sentence length and structure to carry relationships and rhythm; neither a run of clipped sentences nor a chain of loosely attached clauses is a default style.

## 4. Revise for a reason

Apply the following defaults with their limits:

| Default | Preserve the exception when it does useful work |
| --- | --- |
| Prefer active verbs and identifiable actors. | Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, immaterial, or would interrupt the topic. Never invent an actor. |
| State the positive proposition directly. | Keep negation, uncertainty, contrast, understatement, and limitations when they are the meaning. |
| Prefer concrete nouns and exact verbs. | Keep necessary abstractions and technical terms. Add concrete detail only from evidence or an authorized creative brief. |
| Omit needless words. | Keep conditions, distinctions, connective tissue, useful examples, and purposeful repetition. Economy is not minimum length. |
| Prefer familiar, natural diction. | Keep the exact specialist word, apt long word, idiom, or unusual expression when it serves the audience or voice. |
| Reduce empty qualifiers and overstatement. | Retain epistemic qualifiers: “may,” “estimated,” and “in this sample” can be indispensable. |
| Use fresh, coherent imagery sparingly. | Keep a successful image or literal technical metaphor. Do not decorate plain prose just to avoid a cliché. |

For a proposed change, identify the reader's gain: a clearer relationship, accurate emphasis, less ambiguity, stronger rhythm, or a better fit to purpose. If the gain is merely different wording, usually keep the original. Repeat a technical term when it names the same thing; decorative synonym changes can create false distinctions.

Before a substantial cut, identify what information or effect disappears. Decide whether it should be retained, relocated, or removed for the requested purpose. A historical comparison or methodological explanation may earn its space even when the surrounding prose needs tightening. Explain material losses when presenting the edit.

Review voice deliberately. Notice commentary in place of substance ("It is worth understanding…"), manufactured drama ("the crucial distinction"), vague personification, and habitual contrasts, triads, or closing maxims. Watch for repeated summaries and explanations of implications the reader has already grasped. Replace scaffolding with the actual thought; let a point stand when it needs no further explanation. Vary sentence movement with the argument. These are diagnostic patterns, not forbidden constructions or evidence of AI authorship: a contrast, triad, or personification may serve this passage well. Do not manufacture a metaphor, anecdote, statistic, motive, or contrast to make prose seem lively.

Preserve the writer's effective voice: point of view, register, characteristic sentence movement, degree of intimacy, humour, and deliberate repetition. Improve the sentence within that voice. Do not automatically convert literary prose into exposition or scientific restraint into promotional confidence.

## 5. Check meaning separately from smoothness

Compare the revision with the original or brief. Check who did what to whom, when, under which conditions, with what certainty, and on whose evidence. Recheck polarity, quantities, comparisons, exceptions, obligation, causality, and citation attachment. Preserve literal quotations, identifiers, units, and values unless correction is authorized; flag a material factual correction.

Test implications, not just matching words. “Not all” does not mean “none”; association does not establish causation; “only if” does not mean “if”; “not significant” does not establish no effect. A concise revision that changes any of these has failed.

Then read for movement and effect: does the reader know what matters, how each sentence connects, and where emphasis belongs? Are there missing explanations or explanations that smother the point? Is the prose natural for this author and task?

When revising from review findings, reconcile each finding with the result: resolved, deliberately retained with a reason, or still open. Check that each reported defect was actually addressed, revised claims retain their conditions, and replacements introduce no new ambiguity. For comprehensive work, reconcile the coverage record too. Include substantive omissions and unresolved factual questions in the handoff. For critique alone, distinguish proposed remedies from completed edits.

For a short passage, one integrated edit and a separate comparison are usually enough. For a difficult document, separate structure, sentence craft, and final checking. Stop when the brief is satisfied and remaining changes are matters of taste. Do not optimize passive counts, sentence-length targets, readability scores, or quantities of edits as substitutes for quality.

## 6. Return the useful result

Lead with the requested writing in the requested format. Keep routine editorial reasoning internal. Add a short note only for an unresolved ambiguity, a material change, or an explanation the user requested. Distinguish correction, house-style convention, editorial preference, and substantive change when explaining edits. Leave a good passage unchanged when that is the best judgment.
