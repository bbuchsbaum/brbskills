# Scientific and scholarly prose

Read this for manuscripts, abstracts, grant aims, reviews, response letters, and
other writing whose job is to make an argument from evidence. Apply it per
passage: a methods paragraph, a quoted definition, and a discussion argument in
the same paper need different treatment.

Contents: architecture · paper types · sections · claims and evidence ·
sentence work · symptoms · output modes · compression.

## Architecture before polish

The aim is prose that is easier to follow, harder to misread, and calibrated to
the evidence. Within the permitted edit, check these in order:

1. **A live problem early.** The opening presents a tension, gap, conflict,
   puzzle, or methodological limit. Background earns its place by serving that
   problem.
2. **One governing claim** for the paper or section, statable in one sentence.
3. **One job per section and paragraph.** A section that cannot be summarized by
   its job is probably mixing functions; split or reorder it.
4. **Progressive disambiguation.** Order evidence in three steps: the
   demonstration, then the ruling-out of weaker explanations, then the
   implication. For each result, ask what
   skeptical account it answers, and make that visible.
5. **Calibration.** Keep what the evidence shows separate from what it suggests.
6. **Sentences last.** Tighten only after the structure is working.

These are defaults for argumentative scholarship. A methods section, a data
descriptor, a registered-report protocol, or a reply to a specific reviewer point
may legitimately lead with procedure or with the question asked.

For a long or weakly organized draft, write a short change map before revising:
opening problem, governing claim, section jobs, proposed reorderings, and major
cuts or merges. Under a light-edit brief, report structural defects instead of
fixing them.

## Paper types

Identify the type and the target unit (abstract, introduction, results,
discussion, response letter, full draft). Use the flow as a diagnostic, not a
mould.

| Type | Typical flow | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Theory / review | field tension → proposed synthesis or framework → core distinctions → evidence organized by level, source, or theme → limits and open questions | becoming a study catalogue; literature as accumulation rather than synthesis |
| Empirical | problem and prior ambiguity → strategy and why it resolves the ambiguity → main effect → controls and alternative explanations → extension → interpretation and boundary conditions | results subsections that do not answer a stated question |
| Methods | what is wrong with current methods → definition, model, or algorithm → intuition for why it works → worked examples → comparison with prior methods → broader uses | formalism without interpretation |
| Short report | one vivid phenomenon → minimal method → key result → one interpretive step | excess scaffolding that blunts the result |

## Sections

**Introduction.** Paragraph 1: the unresolved problem and why it remains
unresolved. Paragraph 2: what prior work established and what ambiguity remains.
Paragraph 3: the governing claim or strategy and why it addresses the problem.
Add a roadmap only when the paper is complex.

**Results.** For each subsection: state the question, report the result,
interpret it, and say what it rules out or motivates next. When the last step is
missing, the section reads as a list. Keep every claim in the results tied to an
analysis; broader framing belongs in the introduction and discussion. An untested
explanation in the results can stay, labelled as a possibility, under a light
edit; under a rewrite, consider moving it to the discussion and say so.

**Discussion.** Open with the substantive answer, not with commentary on tone,
modesty, or contribution. A strong opening states the answer, names the evidence
pattern that carries it, and gives the most important boundary. Then: which
results carry the most inferential weight; relation to the literature without a
citation dump; what remains unestablished; the next tractable question.

- When evidence is bounded, say so in the claim: "In this sample, …" or "These
  findings suggest …".
- If no single result survives the planned correction or robustness check, do
  not open as if a specific effect were established.
- If the strongest evidence is convergence across analyses, sources, or methods
  rather than one decisive result, say so in the opening paragraph.
- Do not put limitations before the reader knows the answer, or repeat scene
  setting already given in the introduction.
- End by clarifying what the work makes possible, not by restating the abstract.

## Claims and evidence

Help the reader recover the question, the evidence or design, the result, and
the limit of the inference. Treat a qualification, statistical or otherwise, as
part of the finding. Keep these distinctions intact unless the brief authorizes
repairing them, and then disclose the change:

| Preserve | Do not silently substitute |
| --- | --- |
| A result at one level or unit of analysis | The same result at another level |
| How the result was obtained (aggregated, pooled, adjusted, excluded) | A bare level or group label that drops the procedure |
| Failure to detect a difference | Evidence of equivalence or absence |
| Prediction or association | Causal explanation |
| Exploratory result | Confirmatory evidence |
| Estimate under stated conditions or adjustments | Unqualified claim |
| Aggregate or average effect | Effect in every member |
| Consistent with an interpretation | Establishes that interpretation |

Use calibrated verbs and choose them from the evidence, not from the desired tone:
*shows, demonstrates, establishes* → *supports, strongly suggests* → *suggests, is
consistent with* → *raises the possibility* → *does not by itself establish*.
Do not stack weak hedges, and do not delete a hedge that defines scope,
population, or evidential strength. One well-placed qualification can govern a
clearly delimited passage.

Keep a confidence interval with its estimate, a p-value with its test, the
comparison and denominator with a quantity, and each citation with the clause it
supports. Do not infer units, preprocessing, sample sizes, test direction, or
registration status from familiar conventions; flag missing information needed
for reproducibility.

In a discussion, keep the observed result, a possible interpretation, an
alternative explanation, and a proposed test visibly distinct. Improve a weak
argument by restructuring it or narrowing its claim; never manufacture support.

## Sentence work

- In argumentative paragraphs, usually open with a direct claim and put nuance
  right after the claim it limits.
- Follow a major finding with one sentence on what it means.
- Prefer strong nouns and verbs to adjective-heavy inflation.
- Give an abstract claim a concrete anchor nearby: a case, dataset,
  specimen, text, worked example, or vivid phenomenon.
- Track a concept with one term. Do not vary terms for elegance; a new term
  implies a new construct.
- Keep necessary technical vocabulary, including terms that look like ordinary
  words but name a method or construct ("robust regression", "significant",
  "fixed effect"). For a lay audience, explain the term rather than replacing it
  with a looser one.
- Use passive voice when it efficiently foregrounds a procedure; name the actor
  when the actor matters.

## Symptoms worth checking

These often signal a problem. Diagnose the problem before cutting; each item has
legitimate uses.

- Throat-clearing or rhetorical meta-commentary: "importantly", "interestingly",
  "we note that", "it is worth emphasizing", "our claim is modest". Usually
  the scientific claim should simply be stated. Keep the phrase when it marks a
  genuine contrast or a reader would otherwise miss the point's status.
- The same claim restated in slightly different words.
- Figure description with no interpretation.
- Theory paragraphs with no concrete anchor.
- A generic concluding paragraph.
- Method detail inside a conceptual paragraph, unless it changes interpretation.
- Adverbs that tell the reader how to feel instead of sharpening the point.

## Output modes

Choose the smallest output that solves the problem:

- **rewrite:** revised prose.
- **review:** findings first, ordered by how much each one matters, each tied to a passage.
- **outline:** section architecture and paragraph jobs.
- **bridge:** transitions between sections.
- **tighten:** better sentences without changing the argument.

Review checklist: Does the opening contain a live tension? Is there one governing
claim? Does each section have one job? Does each result receive interpretation?
Are limitations explicit and attached to the claims they limit? Does the
conclusion enlarge rather than repeat?

## Compression

Restructure before deleting. Preserve necessary literature and reasoning; cut
redundancy, not substance. When cutting roughly a fifth or more of a section,
flag the result as a substantial compression so the author can check what was
lost.
