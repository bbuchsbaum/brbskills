## Reader-first foundation

**PDF pp. 5–10; afterword p. 75.** Write to help the reader reach the intended thought without struggling through the writer's confusion. Keep both sentence-level correctness and the larger principles of composition visible. Revision remains necessary even for accomplished writers; fluency on the first attempt is not evidence of quality. Economy means that every word serves a purpose, not that every sentence must be short or every subject reduced to an outline. The introduction acknowledges personal preferences and expressly allows broken rules when a compensating merit justifies them. The afterword joins grammatical mechanics to the expression of thought and feeling: correct sentences alone do not constitute good writing.

**Editorial adaptation:** Turn these into reader-facing decisions, not mandatory visible checklists. Do not reproduce the introduction's playful preference for confidence over correctness as an LLM instruction. Accuracy and acknowledged uncertainty take precedence over force.

## R12. Choose a design

*Source: PDF p. 22.*

Give the work a shape fitted to its purpose. The order in which ideas occurred to the writer need not be the order in which readers need them. Plan most expository work, while allowing the design to change when the material warrants it. A sonnet has a fixed frame; an essay has a looser skeleton; a love letter or deliberately wandering informal essay may work without a visible plan. Do not force a single outline on every genre.

## R13. Make the paragraph a unit

*Source: PDF pp. 22–23.*

Give each paragraph a coherent job. A small subject can occupy one paragraph; divide a larger subject into successive topics or steps. A new paragraph signals movement in the thought. The first sentence often introduces the topic or connects it with the previous paragraph, but a gradual opening can work. *The campaign opened with reverses* prepares a paragraph of supporting details. *He picked up the lamp and began to explore* advances narrative without an explicit topic sentence.

Length is functional, not numerical. A paragraph may be one sentence or many. The book discourages repeated one-sentence paragraphs in ordinary exposition, but permits transition sentences and rhetorical pauses. In dialogue, ordinarily start a paragraph when the speaker changes. Rapid narrative may use short paragraphs without topic sentences. Dense blocks can be split to help the eye even when logically coherent; a barrage of tiny paragraphs can be equally distracting. Avoid turning the opening-summary pattern into a mannerism.

## R14. Prefer active voice; choose the subject deliberately

*Source: PDF pp. 23–25.*

Active constructions often make the actor and action clearer: *My first visit to Boston will always be remembered by me* → *I shall always remember my first visit to Boston*. Simply deleting *by me* would change the claim by leaving the remembering agent indefinite.

The book explicitly preserves useful passive voice. *Restoration dramatists are little esteemed today* fits a paragraph about the dramatists; *Modern readers have little esteem for Restoration dramatists* fits one about readers. Choose the subject to sustain the topic and place emphasis.

Look beyond grammatical voice to weak predicates and inert openings:

- *There were many dead leaves lying on the ground* → *Dead leaves covered the ground*.
- *At dawn the crowing of a rooster could be heard* → *The cock's crow came with dawn*.
- *The reason he left college was that his health became impaired* → *Failing health compelled him to leave college*.
- *It was not long before she was sorry for what she had said* → *She soon repented her words*.

These are lessons in informative verbs and sentence focus. Brevity often follows vigor; it is not the sole aim.

**Editorial adaptation:** Do not call every *be* construction passive. Do not invent an agent to make an unknown-agent passive active. Check that a stronger verb does not add force or causal necessity absent from the source.

## R15. State what is meant positively

*Source: PDF pp. 25–26.*

Use a definite assertion instead of a vague denial of its opposite when meaning permits: *not honest → dishonest; did not remember → forgot; paid no attention → ignored; did not have much confidence → distrusted*. *He was not often on time* may become *He usually came late* only if late arrival, rather than absence, is actually meant.

The book distinguishes useful denial and antithesis from evasive negation: *Not charity, but justice*. Negative words such as *never* can be strong and precise. Remove auxiliaries that express no genuine uncertainty: someone known to have died young does not merely *may be ranked among poets who died young*.

**Critical fidelity limit:** The book acknowledges that its revision of vague Shakespeare commentary into *Katharine is disagreeable, Bianca insignificant* is a guess at intention. It also illustrates *can make a good impression* becoming *will make a good impression*. These are **not safe automatic edits**. The operational lesson is to clarify a supported proposition; do not silently replace possibility by certainty, weak dislike by condemnation, lack of proof by falsity, or uncertain meaning by an invented interpretation. Keep necessary *may, might, can, could, should,* and *would*. Scientific and legal negation often carries indispensable scope.

## R16. Use definite, specific, concrete language

*Source: PDF pp. 26–27.*

Choose significant particulars that let the reader understand or imagine what is meant. *A period of unfavorable weather* becomes vivid as *It rained every day for a week*; *He showed satisfaction on receiving a reward* as *He grinned as he pocketed the coin*. These examples assume the particulars are known.

Select revealing detail rather than describing everything. Stafford's menagerie passage works through particular animals, behavior, drink, voices, and physical features; it creates a scene rather than naming “a colorful character.” In exposition, connect general propositions to instances. Spencer's general link between cruel amusements and severe punishments gains force through named activities and punishments. The Orwell/Ecclesiastes contrast shows abstract *success or failure in competitive activities* dissolving the race, battle, bread, and human agents that made the original intelligible.

**Editorial adaptation:** Never invent rain duration, a coin, a grin, an exact mechanism, a sample size, or a vivid anecdote to satisfy this advice. In editing, recover supported specifics or expose the missing detail. Retain abstractions and technical terms that express necessary generalizations; give examples when useful.

## R17. Omit needless words

*Source: PDF pp. 28–29.*

Require each word, sentence, and detail to do work. Do not confuse economy with clipped prose or omitted reasoning.

| Wordy pattern | Compact form, where equivalent |
| --- | --- |
| *the question as to whether* | *whether; the question whether* |
| *there is no doubt but that* | *no doubt; doubtless* |
| *used for fuel purposes* | *used for fuel* |
| *he is a man who* | *he* |
| *in a hasty manner* | *hastily* |
| *this is a subject that* | *this subject* |
| *Her story is a strange one.* | *Her story is strange.* |
| *the reason why is that* | *because* |
| *owing to the fact that* | *because; since* |
| *in spite of the fact that* | *although; though* |
| *call your attention to the fact that* | *remind you; notify you* |
| *I was unaware of the fact that* | *I did not know; I was unaware that* |
| *the fact that he had not succeeded* | *his failure* |
| *the fact that I had arrived* | *my arrival* |
| *his cousin, who is a member of the firm* | *his cousin, a member of the firm* |
| *Trafalgar, which was Nelson's last battle* | *Trafalgar, Nelson's last battle* |

A paragraph may be wordy because it explains one relationship in a chain of tiny sentences. The book's Macbeth example compresses ambition, prophecy, encouragement, murder, and succession into one organized sentence rather than narrating each link separately.

**Editorial adaptation:** Preserve tense, aspect, scope, useful transitions, and the requested level of detail. *His failure* may be too blunt or misleading when the source says only that he had not yet succeeded. A summary may drop detail by design; an ordinary copyedit may not.

## R18. Vary sentence architecture

*Source: PDF pp. 29–30.*

Avoid a paragraph in which every sentence consists of a main clause followed by another clause introduced with *and, but, who, which, when, where,* or *while*. The concert-report example repeats a mechanical two-part rhythm: concert/audience; soloist/orchestra; former/latter; interest/future plan.

Replace enough of these with simple sentences, semicolon-linked clauses, periodic sentences, and differently balanced multi-clause structures. Choose the form that expresses the actual relationship. Loose sentences can be easy and natural; an occasional one relieves excessive formality. Forster's contrasting passage varies length, fragments, parallel constructions, and clause relations while sustaining one thought.

**Editorial adaptation:** Seek movement dictated by meaning, not arbitrary length alternation. Avoid a new template in which every paragraph must contain one short sentence, a triad, and a final maxim.

## R19. Parallel ideas, parallel forms

*Source: PDF pp. 30–31.*

Make elements similar in function recognizably similar in grammar. Do not vary terminology or construction merely to avoid repetition. *Formerly science was taught by textbooks, while now the laboratory method is employed* → *Formerly science was taught by textbooks; now it is taught in laboratories*.

Either apply a shared article/preposition once or repeat it consistently: *the French, Italians, Spanish, and Portuguese* or *the French, the Italians, the Spanish, and the Portuguese*; *in spring, summer, or winter* or repeat *in* for each. Keep different prepositions when the words require them: *disagreement with and scorn for the position*.

Align correlative constructions:

- *both a long ceremony and very tedious* → *both long and tedious*.
- *not for words but action* → *not for words but for action*.
- *Either you must grant the request or incur ill will* → *You must either grant the request or incur ill will*.
- *first, the injustice; second, that it is unconstitutional* → two nouns or two *that* clauses.

For a large set of coordinate points, group related points or use a table. Parallelism need not mean twenty monotonous sentences.

## R20. Keep related words together

*Source: PDF pp. 31–33.*

Put modifiers near the words they govern and separate accidental attachments. The reader should not have to repair the syntax mentally.

| Ambiguity | Repair principle |
| --- | --- |
| *a stain in the rug that was right in the center* | *a stain right in the center of the rug* |
| *call your mother and tell her about dinner for two dollars* | Put *For two dollars* beside the phone-call proposition. |
| *samples from eighteen men frozen in a tank* | Make *samples* the explicit subject of *were frozen*. |
| *Toni Morrison, in Beloved, writes...* | *In Beloved, Toni Morrison writes...* |
| *A dog, if you fail to discipline him, becomes a pest* | *Unless disciplined, a dog becomes a pest*. |
| *a stir in the audience that suggested disapproval* | *A stir that suggested disapproval swept the audience*. |
| *articles about adventures in Spain, which were published...* | Put publication beside *articles*; do not attach *which* to Spain. |
| *a proposal to amend the Act, which was criticized* | Say whether the proposal or the Act was criticized. |
| *All the members were not present.* | *Not all the members were present*, if that is the intended scope. |
| *She only found two mistakes.* | *She found only two mistakes*, if number is the limitation. |
| *hoped members would donate at yesterday's meeting* | Move the meeting time beside *said* when it dates the statement. |
| Lecture announcement with time, place, invitation, and title jumbled | Group time/place/title; put *The public is invited* in its own sentence. |

Ordinarily keep the subject near its principal verb. An appositive or relative clause may intervene without trouble; deliberate suspension can create emphasis. Put a relative pronoun near its antecedent, sometimes after the entire noun phrase, but not where it creates two plausible antecedents. A clarifying appositive can intervene: *the duke, his brother, who...*.

**Editorial adaptation:** Where two readings remain possible, do not choose one silently unless context settles it. The source's Harrison example on PDF p. 32 is itself not a safe model: its rearranged *who became President in 1889* follows the grandfather's name. Use an explicitly named subject or a separate sentence instead.

## R21. Keep summary tense coherent

*Source: PDF p. 34.*

Use present tense for dramatic action and ordinarily for fiction summaries; past tense is acceptable where natural. Mark events earlier than the chosen narrative time with present perfect or past perfect as appropriate. Do not alternate tenses without a temporal reason. A present-tense account can properly report an earlier event in the past: *The friar confesses that he performed the marriage*.

Signal once that a passage is another author's account; do not begin every sentence *he says, she states, the author then adds*. Restore attribution when it would otherwise become unclear. In literary criticism, make an argument supported by selected details rather than retelling the plot and occasionally commenting. Across several works, organize by conclusions and comparisons instead of reflexively taking each work in chronological order.

## R22. Put emphasis where the reader feels it

*Source: PDF pp. 34–35.*

The end of a sentence often carries the main new idea. Reorder background and main point accordingly:

- *Humanity has hardly advanced in fortitude, though it has advanced in other ways* → *Humanity has advanced in many ways, but hardly in fortitude*.
- *This steel makes razors because it is hard* → *Because it is hard, this steel is used for razors*, when use rather than hardness is the point.

A periodic sentence delays the principal statement until supporting material has prepared it. The beginning is also prominent, especially when a normally later element is moved forward: *Deceit she could never forgive*; *Home is the sailor*. An opening subject is not automatically emphatic; inversion can place the important subject at the end: *Through the valley flowed a winding stream*.

The same principle scales upward: the closing sentence of a paragraph and the closing paragraph of a piece deserve material that carries their weight.

**Editorial adaptation:** Preserve natural topic continuity and qualifying information. Do not drag every caveat to an inconspicuous position or manufacture a ringing final line.
