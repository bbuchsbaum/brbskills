# Orwell: meaning before phrasing

Source: the supplied text of George Orwell's *Politics and the English Language* (1946). This is a compressed interpretation with short source examples. Sections labelled **Application** adapt the essay for present-day drafting and editing. Use it as a supplement to Strunk and White.

## The central lesson

Choose language to express a thought; do not let familiar language decide what the thought is. Orwell describes a feedback loop: unclear thought produces bad prose, and inherited verbal habits make unclear thought easier. An edit should expose the proposition, not simply improve its sound.

His concern reaches beyond ornament. Abstract and euphemistic phrasing can hide an action, its agent, its victims, or its consequences. Ask what happened in terms that a reader could identify. Do not mistake an elevated register for evidence or neutrality.

He explicitly rejects antiquarian English, artificial simplicity, obligatory colloquialism, and the idea that every abstract word is meaningless. His immediate subject is language used to communicate thought, not a universal recipe for literature.

## Four habits to diagnose

| Habit | Diagnosis and source examples | Editorial move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dying metaphors | Worn images save the writer from selecting exact language: “toe the line,” “Achilles' heel,” “swan song.” Mixed images reveal that their physical implications have not been considered. | State the literal relationship, or use one apt image. Distinguish a fresh image from a conventional term that now functions as ordinary language. |
| Verbal false limbs | A vague verb plus an abstract noun displaces a precise verb: “make contact with,” “have the effect of,” “serve the purpose of.” Inflated connectors and ceremonial endings extend the sentence without developing it. | When the meanings match, write “contact,” “affect,” or “serve”; replace a long connective with “because,” “if,” or “about.” Recover the actual action before selecting the verb. |
| Pretentious diction | Learned, foreign, martial, or apparently scientific language supplies borrowed dignity. Words such as “historic” or “inevitable” can replace an argument; “utilize” may add nothing to “use.” | Keep the term that identifies the thing accurately. Remove prestige that the claim has not earned. Technical language is useful when it preserves a real distinction. |
| Meaningless or unstable words | Praise, blame, and abstract labels can circulate without agreed referents. Orwell contrasts critics' “living” and “dead” qualities and discusses elastic political terms such as “democracy.” | Ask what observation, criterion, action, or contrast the word identifies here. Define the operative sense or show its basis. Do not replace one vague evaluation with another. |

**Application:** These are questions about function, not a blacklist. “Effect,” “objective,” “phenomenon,” and “predict” can be exact scientific terms. Changing them merely because Orwell listed examples would destroy the distinction between pretension and precision.

## What the examples teach

### The five opening specimens

1. **Laski:** accumulated negatives and a mistaken word obscure or reverse the proposition. Parse the claim before smoothing it; if the intended claim is unknown, flag it.
2. **Hogben:** several incompatible figures and conspicuously learned diction make an argument for ordinary idiom needlessly obscure. Test whether the images can be pictured together and whether the learned word is accurate.
3. **The psychology passage:** connected abstract nouns create an appearance of argument without making the relationship intelligible. Supply an actual proposition only if context supports it.
4. **The political pamphlet:** a recognizable view is smothered by stock denunciations. State the claim and its grounds instead of accumulating declarations of allegiance.
5. **The BBC letter:** theatrical imagery, alliteration, and emotional noise displace particulars. Ask what change is being proposed and why.

### Ecclesiastes and its inflated translation

The original makes uncertainty concrete through races, battles, bread, wealth, and favour. Orwell's parody compresses these into abstract competitive outcomes. It is shorter in words but heavier in syllables and poorer in meaning, imagery, and cadence.

**Lesson:** A word-count reduction can make a passage worse. Preserve the examples and relations that let the reader grasp the thought. Compression succeeds when it removes verbal labour, not substantive content.

### Mixed imagery and automatic phrasing

An octopus singing a swan song and a jackboot entering a melting pot cannot sustain coherent pictures. Phrases such as “achieve a radical transformation” and “lay the foundations” can arrive as ready-made sequences before the writer has specified the change.

**Application:** Replace “The initiative will lay the foundations for a radical transformation” with what the initiative will actually do—if the brief provides that information. Otherwise request the missing substance or mark it as unspecified. Do not invent a deliverable to rescue the sentence.

### Euphemism and hidden agency

Orwell contrasts “pacification” with violent attacks on villages; “transfer of population” with people driven from their homes; and “elimination of unreliable elements” with imprisonment and killing. The wording conceals the action and its human effects. His invented defence of repression demonstrates how qualifications and abstract nouns can give brutality an administrative surface.

**Application:** Recover supported actors, actions, and consequences. If only “The records were deleted” is known, keep the unknown agent unknown. Replacing an evasive phrase with an unsupported accusation is not clarity. Do not assume that every abstraction or passive construction is deliberately deceptive.

### Repeated negatives

The comic sequence of a “not unblack” dog, a “not unsmall” rabbit, and a “not ungreen” field exposes empty double-negative ornament.

**Application:** Distinguish ornament from logic and understatement. “Not impossible” is weaker than “likely”; “not unreasonable” may express guarded approval. Preserve the intended degree of commitment.

## The six rules, used with judgment

1. Avoid familiar, exhausted figures when they contribute no live meaning.
2. Prefer the shorter word when it conveys the meaning equally well.
3. Remove a word when nothing useful is lost.
4. Prefer active voice when it improves the sentence.
5. Prefer an everyday equivalent to needless jargon or a foreign phrase.
6. Override these preferences rather than produce distorted or graceless language.

These paraphrases deliberately preserve the sixth rule's force. The essay states the earlier rules more absolutely, but also rejects mechanical simplicity and concedes that a writer could follow every rule and still write badly.

## A practical check

Ask, internally: What exactly is the claim? Which words convey it? Would an image clarify it, and is that image coherent? Can the sentence be shorter without losing anything? Is its awkwardness avoidable? What will a reader infer?

For a vague sentence, first identify the missing substance. For an inflated sentence, recover the nouns and verbs that bear meaning. For an evasive sentence, make the supported agency and consequences visible. Finish by checking that the clearer sentence is still true to the evidence and the author's intended stance.
