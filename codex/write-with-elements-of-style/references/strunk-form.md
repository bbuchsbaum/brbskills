Examples are condensed, normalized, or adapted from the supplied text. An arrow illustrates a revision; it does not license the substitution in every context. **Editorial adaptation** labels guidance added to apply the source responsibly.

## F01. Colloquialisms

*Source: PDF p. 36.*

Use a fitting colloquialism naturally. Do not put a slang expression in quotation marks merely to distance the author from it or signal superiority. Quotation marks remain appropriate for actual quotation, a discussed term, or another genuine quoting function.

## F02. Exclamations

*Source: PDF p. 36.*

Do not add exclamation marks to make a simple assertion seem exciting. *It was a wonderful show.* ordinarily suffices. Reserve the mark for true exclamation or a command where the tone requires it: *What a show! Halt!* **Editorial adaptation:** Allow normal conversational warmth where the genre supports it; assess the effect rather than counting marks.

## F03. Headings

*Source: PDF p. 36.*

Leave useful visual space around headings; omit an ordinary terminal period. Retain a question or exclamation mark if integral to the title. The book's quarter-page space above a first-page manuscript title was intended for typesetting instructions. **Editorial adaptation:** Follow the actual template or venue instead of imposing this obsolete production layout.

## F04. Hyphens

*Source: PDF pp. 36–37.*

Use hyphens to show the intended grouping in compound modifiers: *leisure class* but *leisure-class pursuits*; *round-the-island race*. Consult a dictionary for established closed compounds: *waterfowl, bedroom/bedchamber, wildlife, bellboy*. Do not preserve a hyphen merely because an older spelling used one. A hyphen only visibly connects adjacent words; *Chattanooga News-Free Press* accidentally suggests a press devoid of news. **Editorial adaptation:** Apply current dictionary and house style; not every multiword premodifier needs a hyphen.

## F05. Margins

*Source: PDF p. 37.*

Keep margins visually balanced and allow space for expected annotation. **Editorial adaptation:** Let the document template, review medium, and accessibility requirements determine dimensions.

## F06. Numerals

*Source: PDF p. 37.*

Use figures or the appropriate Roman form for dates and numbered items: *August 9, 1988; Part XII; Rule 3*. The book often spells dates and numbers in dialogue to represent speech: *August ninth; Chapter Twelve*. **Editorial adaptation:** Follow the requested scientific, technical, or publication number style; preserve every quantitative value and unit.

## F07. Parentheses

*Source: PDF p. 37.*

Punctuate the surrounding sentence as though the parenthesis were absent: *I went yesterday (my third attempt), but she had left*. Inside an embedded parenthesis, omit a terminal period but retain a necessary question or exclamation mark: *He declares (why doubt him?) that...*. A complete detached parenthetical sentence puts its period inside the closing parenthesis.

## F08. Quotations

*Source: PDF pp. 37–38.*

A complete introductory assertion may take a colon before a supporting quotation. Direct speech or a quotation grammatically attached to an attribution commonly takes a comma: *Twain says, “...”*; *“I can't attend,” she said*. The book uses US typographic placement of commas and periods inside quotation marks. Distinguish displayed extracts typographically with a new indented block and omit outer quotation marks unless part of the quoted content.

The book distinguishes indirect report introduced by *that* from direct quotation, and omits quotation marks around familiar proverbial/literary phrases. **Editorial adaptation:** Preserve exact quoted words and give required attribution. A verb followed by *that* does not prohibit a genuinely direct quotation within the clause. Modern block-quote thresholds and punctuation follow the venue. Familiarity does not remove scholarly citation obligations.

## F09. References

*Source: PDF p. 39.*

Keep exact references available without cluttering the sentence. A recurring abbreviated title needs an explicit key; parenthetical citations or notes often serve better than writing location labels into the prose. The book compresses act/scene/line references, as in *(IV.ii.14)*, and uses chapter/verse notation for scripture. **Editorial adaptation:** Use the required citation system, not these examples as a universal format. Never shorten a reference until it becomes ambiguous or separate a citation from the claim it supports.

## F10. Syllabication

*Source: PDF p. 39.*

When a word must break across a line, use a dictionary's permissible division rather than guessing. **Editorial adaptation:** Let modern layout software hyphenate with the correct language settings; avoid hard-coded line-break hyphens in editable prose.

## F11. Titles

*Source: PDF p. 39.*

Follow the publication's convention for italics, capitalization, and quotation marks; the book prefers italicized literary titles in scholarly writing. A preceding author's possessive displaces an initial article in the examples: *A Tale of Two Cities* → *Dickens's Tale of Two Cities*; *The Age of Innocence* → *Wharton's Age of Innocence*. **Editorial adaptation:** Distinguish books from shorter works and respect a mandated style guide. The historical use of underlining to instruct a compositor is not necessary in digital output.
