# Diction: the complete Chapter IV guide

Use these entries to resolve a word, idiom, or distinction in context. Preserve useful meaning rather than replacing words mechanically. The chapter itself warns that many entries concern weak style rather than bad grammar, that definite statements often work better than synonym swaps, and that usage has no final lawgiver.

Source: *The Elements of Style*, fourth edition, Chapter IV, supplied PDF pp. 40–60. Page numbers identify 1-based PDF positions, not the book's printed pagination. Examples are compressed from that source unless marked **adapted**. Entry numbers below are coverage identifiers, not rule numbers in the book. **Adaptation** labels identify practical editorial qualifications added for this skill; they do not claim to reproduce the book's position.

## Editorial treatment

Follow the brief, genre, English variety, reader, and required house style. Treat historical restrictions as contextual recommendations; consult a current dictionary or the venue's guidance if a disputed convention materially affects the result. Do not impose generic *he*, reject singular *they*, strip legitimate technical terms, or treat older preferences about *hopefully*, restrictive *which*, split infinitives, *data*, *gotten*, or word formation as universal grammar laws.

Make a specific substitution only when the source supports it. *Contact* does not establish that someone phoned; *facilities* does not establish a toilet; a contributory *factor* is not necessarily the sole cause. Preserve the scope, agency, and evidential stance conveyed by the original wording.

## A through C

### 1. Aggravate. Irritate

**Source: PDF p. 40.** Book distinction: *aggravate* makes an existing trouble worse; *irritate* annoys or chafes. **Adapted:** aggravate an injury; irritate a colleague. Preserve useful precision; do not automatically mark every broader idiomatic use erroneous.

### 2. All right

**Source: PDF p. 40.** Two-word spelling; a detached conversational *all right* can mean agreement or permission. Treat spelling choice as the book's conventional preference, not a reason to rewrite quoted speech.

### 3. Allude

**Source: PDF p. 40.** Allude indirectly; refer specifically; elude a pursuer. **Adapted:** allude to a myth / refer to a particular chapter / elude capture.

### 4. Allusion

**Source: PDF p. 40.** An allusion is an indirect reference; an illusion is a false impression or unreal image.

### 5. Alternate. Alternative

**Source: PDF p. 40.** Book distinction: *alternate* means every other or a substitute; *alternative* implies a choice. Source contrast: a flooded road leaves no alternative, so travelers take the alternate route. Do not enforce the book's “two possibilities” limit as a general rule.

### 6. Among. Between

**Source: PDF p. 40.** *Among* for a group; *between* for distinct individual relationships, even with more than two participants. Divide money among four players; an agreement between six heirs. Do not teach “between only for two.”

### 7. And/or

**Source: PDF p. 41.** State the logic explicitly when the slash confuses scope: “stealing and/or cheating” → “stealing or cheating or both.” Preserve inclusiveness when rewriting; a bare *or* may leave it unclear.

### 8. Anticipate

**Source: PDF p. 41.** Prefer *expect* for bare prediction; *anticipate* can imply advance action. “I anticipated he would look older” → “I expected…”; a person who anticipates a market rise may buy beforehand. Do not invent such action.

### 9. Anybody

**Source: PDF p. 41.** *Anybody* means any person; *any body* refers to a corpse, form, or group. Same distinction for *everybody/every body*, *nobody/no body*, *somebody/some body*.

### 10. Anyone

**Source: PDF p. 41.** *Anyone* = anybody; *any one* = any single person or thing.

### 11. As good or better than

**Source: PDF p. 41.** Restore both comparative constructions: “as good or better than his” → “as good as his, or better” / “as good as his, if not better.” Preserve the intended comparison.

### 12. As to whether

**Source: PDF p. 41.** Usually simply *whether*.

### 13. As yet

**Source: PDF p. 41.** Usually *yet*: “No agreement has been reached as yet” → “No agreement has yet been reached.” Exception at sentence opening: *Yet he failed* means nevertheless; *As yet he has failed* means so far. Preserve the temporal/adversative distinction.

### 14. Being

**Source: PDF p. 42.** Cut redundant *being* after *regarded as*: “regarded as being the best dancer” → “regarded as the best dancer.”

### 15. But

**Source: PDF p. 42.** Book reductions: “no doubt but that” → “no doubt that”; “could not help but see” → “could not help seeing.” Avoid a chain of reversals; consider *although* or reordering. Source pattern: country has resources **but** is unprepared; **but** builds army → country is unprepared **but** has resources; next sentence reports army. Do not turn the idiomatic *cannot help but* into a mandatory correction.

### 16. Can

**Source: PDF p. 42.** Book reserves *can* for ability and *may* for permission. **Adaptation:** treat this as a useful formal distinction, not a ban on ordinary permission questions; preserve possibility, permission, and obligation when wording matters.

### 17. Care less

**Source: PDF p. 42.** The literal dismissal is *couldn't care less*. Omitting *not* reverses literal meaning; preserve intentional idiom or characterization when relevant.

### 18. Case

**Source: PDF p. 42.** Cut empty frames: “In many cases, the rooms lacked…” → “Many rooms lacked…”; “It has rarely been the case that any mistake…” → “Few mistakes…” Keep *case* for an actual case, scenario, or unit.

### 19. Certainly

**Source: PDF p. 42.** Repeated use as a universal intensifier becomes a mannerism. Keep it when certainty or concession contributes meaning; do not convert uncertainty to certainty.

### 20. Character

**Source: PDF p. 43.** “acts of a hostile character” → “hostile acts.” Keep it when character itself is the topic.

### 21. Claim (verb)

**Source: PDF p. 43.** Book favors the possession/entitlement sense and objects to using it for *declare/maintain/charge*: “claimed she was sole heir” is defensible; “claimed he knew how” → “declared…” **Adaptation:** do not enforce the old restriction or erase attribution/doubt carried by *claim*.

### 22. Clever

**Source: PDF p. 43.** Source notes a historically distinct horse sense (“good-natured”) versus human ingenuity. General lesson: check domain-specific meanings; do not force this historical horse usage onto current prose.

### 23. Compare

**Source: PDF p. 43.** Book favors *compare to* for likening unlike things and *compare with* for examining comparable ones: life to a pilgrimage; Congress with Parliament; Paris to ancient Athens but with modern London. Treat as guidance on intended relation, not a universal lexical test.

### 24. Comprise

**Source: PDF p. 43.** Whole comprises parts; parts constitute the whole: a zoo comprises animals; animals constitute a zoo. For straightforward editing, *includes* or *consists of* can remove distraction; preserve whether the parts exhaust the whole.

### 25. Consider

**Source: PDF p. 43.** *Believe to be*: “consider him as competent” → “consider him competent.” *Examine in a role*: “considered Eisenhower first as soldier, second as administrator” keeps *as*.

### 26. Contact

**Source: PDF p. 43.** Book rejects the verb as vague and proposes phone/find/meet/get in touch. **Adaptation:** retain ordinary *contact* when method is unspecified or irrelevant; choose a specific verb only if the source supports that method.

### 27. Cope

**Source: PDF p. 43.** Book asks formal prose to name what is coped with: “they would cope” → “cope with the situation.” **Adaptation:** standalone *cope* need not be corrected when its object is clear; add context only if needed.

### 28. Currently

**Source: PDF p. 44.** Present tense often makes it redundant. Source: “currently reviewing” → “at this moment reviewing” for actual temporal emphasis. **Adaptation:** ordinarily “reviewing” is enough; preserve a meaningful current/past/planned contrast and do not add unsupported immediacy.

## D through G

### 29. Data

**Source: PDF p. 44.** Book prefers plural agreement while acknowledging growing singular use: “The data is…” → “These data are…”. **Adaptation:** choose consistent usage suited to discipline/house style; neither number by itself establishes accuracy.

### 30. Different than

**Source: PDF p. 44.** Book prefers *different from*, *other than*, or *unlike*. Treat this as an editorial convention; don't make a syntactically worse recast to avoid an idiomatic construction.

### 31. Disinterested

**Source: PDF p. 44.** Preserve the useful distinction between impartial and uninterested. An impartial judge is disinterested; a bored observer is uninterested.

### 32. Divided into

**Source: PDF p. 44.** Distinguish partition from composition: plays divided into acts; poems composed of stanzas; a halved apple divided into sections, but composed of seeds, flesh, and skin. The source acknowledges a blurred boundary.

### 33. Due to

**Source: PDF p. 44.** Book prefers *because of* for an adverbial cause and *due to* for “attributable to”: “lost due to carelessness” → “lost because of carelessness”; “losses due to fires” remains. **Adaptation:** no universal grammatical ban; preserve the cause and avoid unnecessary churn.

### 34. Each and every one

**Source: PDF p. 44.** Usually *every one* or *all*: “a lesson to each and every one of us” → “a lesson to us all.” Source exempts dialogue; emphasis may also be intentional.

### 35. Effect

**Source: PDF pp. 44–45.** Noun = result; verb = bring about; *affect* = influence. “Delicate/subtle/charming effects” may hide what happened in a work of art; specify the actual perceptible result, if known. Keep technical meanings.

### 36. Enormity

**Source: PDF p. 45.** Book reserves it for monstrous wickedness, not size. **Adaptation:** use *magnitude/size* when a reader might infer moral horror; do not treat etymology as a complete modern usage test.

### 37. Enthuse

**Source: PDF p. 45.** Book dislikes the verb: “was enthused” → “was enthusiastic”; “enthused about” → “expressed enthusiasm about.” **Adaptation:** this is taste/history; choose natural wording without obligatory expansion.

### 38. Etc

**Source: PDF p. 45.** Do not hide important missing particulars. Least objectionable for the immaterial tail of a nearly complete, known list or quotation; redundant after *such as/for example*. Book discourages it in formal writing. Never turn an illustrative list into an exhaustive one during deletion.

### 39. Fact

**Source: PDF p. 45.** Reserve factual presentation for verifiable propositions; distinguish evaluative judgment. Dates and a metal's melting temperature differ from “greatest general” or “delightful climate.” Do not use *fact* as rhetorical certification.

### 40. Facility

**Source: PDF p. 45.** Name the thing where useful: “wooden facility” → “wooden schoolhouse”; “warden of new facility” → “warden of new prison.” **Adaptation:** only add the specific noun if supported; a genuinely mixed/unspecified facility can stay one.

### 41. Factor

**Source: PDF pp. 45–46.** Replace a vague carrier with the relationship: training was a factor in victory → won through better training; air power is an important factor → plays a larger part. **Adaptation:** do not turn one contributory factor into the sole cause or discard technical *factor*.

### 42. Farther. Further

**Source: PDF p. 46.** Book recommends *farther* for physical distance, *further* for development, time, or quantity: chase a ball farther; pursue a subject further. It acknowledges interchange.

### 43. Feature

**Source: PDF p. 46.** State the actual noteworthy detail rather than announcing a feature: “A feature worth mentioning was Allison's singing” → say what/how she sang, **if known**. Book also dislikes the verb meaning a special attraction. **Adaptation:** retain legitimate product/statistical/editorial senses; never invent descriptive details.

### 44. Finalize

**Source: PDF p. 46; V.21 pp. 73–74.** Source objects that it can blur “terminate” and “put into final form.” Specify the intended action when ambiguous. No global ban on an otherwise apt current word.

### 45. Fix

**Source: PDF p. 46.** Source acknowledges established American *arrange/prepare/mend* but prefers make firm/place definitely. **Adaptation:** preserve natural dialect and context; etymology is not a mandatory replacement rule.

### 46. Flammable

**Source: PDF p. 46.** Source explains that *flammable* and *inflammable* both mean combustible and that the clearer warning saves lives; oddly it prefers *inflammable* outside such warnings. **Adaptation:** retain the safety lesson and prefer the unambiguous label where needed; do not import the dated social commentary or a perverse preference for ambiguity.

### 47. Folk

**Source: PDF p. 46.** Book prefers collective *folk*, calls *folks* colloquial, and recasts “her folks” as “her father and mother.” **Adaptation:** preserve informal voice and inclusive/accurate family reference; never infer a person's family composition.

### 48. Fortuitous

**Source: PDF p. 46.** Intended distinction: happening by chance versus fortunate/lucky. **OCR defect:** the attached entry reads “Limited to what to be used for fortunate or lucky”; the missing phrase cannot be quoted as intact source. Preserve the chance/luck distinction as a marked reconstruction.

### 49. Get

**Source: PDF pp. 46–47.** Book cuts possessive *have got*: “has not got any sense” → “has no sense”; prose says prefer *got* to *gotten*. **OCR/layout caveat:** the following pair prints *got* before *gotten*, at odds with the scan's claimed bad→good convention. **Adaptation:** keep British/American distinctions and semantic differences among possession/acquisition; no blanket ban.

### 50. Gratuitous

**Source: PDF p. 47.** Means unearned/unwarranted; a gratuitous insult is undeserved. Do not confuse with merely free of charge in a context where the evaluative sense matters.

## H through L

### 51. He is a man who

**Source: PDF p. 47.** Remove empty categorization: “He is a man who is ambitious” → “He is ambitious”; “Vermont is a state that attracts…” → “Vermont attracts…”. Keep category information when informative.

### 52. Hopefully

**Source: PDF p. 47.** Book permits manner “with hope” but rejects sentence-level “it is hoped.” **Adaptation:** do not enforce that historical objection. Resolve actual ambiguity or identify whose hope matters; ordinary sentence-level use can remain.

### 53. However

**Source: PDF p. 47.** Book prefers medial adversative *however*: “However, we reached camp” → “At last, however, we reached camp.” Initial *however* can mean “in whatever way/to whatever extent”: “However you advise him…”; “However discouraging…”. **Adaptation:** initial “However,” is not inherently erroneous; use position and punctuation to clarify relation.

### 54. Illusion

**Source: PDF p. 47.** Cross-reference to **Allusion**; preserve both search keys.

### 55. Imply. Infer

**Source: PDF p. 47.** To imply is to suggest; to infer is to deduce from evidence. Farming implies early rising; an observer infers that the farmer rises early. Check who supplies the indication and who draws the conclusion.

### 56. Importantly

**Source: PDF p. 48.** Book suggests “What's more” or “More important” instead of “More importantly.” **Adaptation:** the useful lesson is to state the consequence rather than announce importance; no automatic grammatical correction of *importantly*.

### 57. In regard to

**Source: PDF p. 48.** Book prefers singular *regard*; *as regards* is correct. Often *about* is clearer if it preserves the relation.

### 58. In the last analysis

**Source: PDF p. 48.** A worn-out summary marker; omit or state the analytical conclusion.

### 59. Inside of. Inside

**Source: PDF p. 48.** Source allows *inside of* for “in less than”: “Inside of five minutes I'll be inside the bank.” Else drop *of* when it contributes nothing; preserve dialect and natural idiom.

### 60. Insightful

**Source: PDF p. 48.** Avoid inflated praise; reserve for genuinely penetrating insight. Source downgrades a routine “insightful remark” to *perceptive*. **Adaptation:** describing the actual insight is stronger than exchanging praise labels.

### 61. In terms of

**Source: PDF p. 48.** Often padding: “unattractive in terms of salary” → “The salary made the job unattractive.” Keep precise technical expressions, comparison bases, and definitions when this phrase supplies them.

### 62. Interesting

**Source: PDF p. 48.** Demonstrate interest rather than announce it: “An interesting story is told…” → tell it; “it is interesting to recall…” → supply the relevant fact. The same applies to prefacing something as *funny*.

### 63. Irregardless

**Source: PDF p. 49.** Prefer conventional *regardless* in edited prose; preserve quoted/character speech when intentional.

### 64. -ize

**Source: PDF p. 49.** Source approves *summarize/fraternize/harmonize/fertilize*, rejects then-new *containerize/prioritize/finalize*, and prefers *use* to *utilize*. **Adaptation:** judge necessity, precision, and register; productive morphology is not intrinsically wrong. Do not ban established technical verbs because of the suffix.

### 65. Kind of

**Source: PDF p. 49.** Keep literal classification (“a kind of fossil resin”); source discourages hedge uses meaning rather/something like outside familiar style. **Adaptation:** preserve useful approximation and speech rhythm; distinguish a filler from a meaningful hedge.

### 66. Lay

**Source: PDF p. 49.** *Lay* is transitive; *lie* intransitive. Lay an egg; lie down; yesterday lay down. Forms: **lie–lay–lain–lying**; **lay–laid–laid–laying**. Source recognizes slang exception.

### 67. Leave

**Source: PDF p. 49.** Do not confuse with *let*: “Leave it stand” → “Let it stand”; “Leave go” → “Let go.” Preserve deliberate dialect.

### 68. Less

**Source: PDF p. 49.** Distinguish magnitude and count: fewer workers; troubles less severe versus troubles fewer in number. **Adaptation:** do not mechanically apply the count rule to conventional quantities, measurements, money, time, or idioms without checking meaning.

### 69. Like

**Source: PDF pp. 49–50.** Book prefers *as* before clauses/phrases: “like in the old days” → “as in…”; “like a baby should” → “as…”. **Adaptation:** contextual formality, not a universal ban; retain ordinary speech and intentional voice. Omit source's disparaging social labels.

### 70. Line. Along these lines

**Source: PDF p. 50.** Overused framework: “spoke along the same lines” → “spoke to the same effect”; studying along the line of French literature → studying French literature. The latter source pair is garbled; this is a normalized reconstruction.

### 71. Literal. Literally

**Source: PDF p. 50.** Do not accidentally certify a metaphor: “a literal flood of abuse” → “a flood…”; “literally dead with fatigue” → “almost dead…”. **Adaptation:** retain deliberate hyperbole where genre permits, but avoid factual confusion.

### 72. Loan

**Source: PDF p. 50.** Book prefers noun *loan*, verb *lend*. **Adaptation:** lexical preference rather than universal prohibition of the verb; check idiom and house style.

## M through P

### 73. Meaningful

**Source: PDF p. 50.** Vague approval needs content: “meaningful contribution” → “contribution counted heavily”; “meaningful curriculum changes” → actual improvements. **Adaptation:** do not replace vague praise with an unsupported claim that the changes improved anything; request/retain the missing criterion if needed.

### 74. Memento

**Source: PDF p. 51.** Not *momento*.

### 75. Most

**Source: PDF p. 51.** Formal prose: *almost everybody*, *almost all the time*, not colloquial *most everybody/all the time*. Do not confuse the quantifier *most* with *almost* when their scopes differ.

### 76. Nature

**Source: PDF p. 51.** “acts of a hostile nature” → “hostile acts.” Specify what “poems about nature” concerns—scenery, rural life, sunset, wilderness, squirrels—if the content supports it. Retain a general category when generality is intended.

### 77. Nauseous. Nauseated

**Source: PDF p. 51.** Book assigns causing nausea to *nauseous*, feeling it to *nauseated*. **Adaptation:** do not treat the older distinction as a universal current restriction; prefer unambiguous wording when necessary.

### 78. Nice

**Source: PDF p. 51.** General praise is often indistinct; useful precise sense is “a nice distinction.” Specify pleasant weather, kindness, or precision when supported; preserve ordinary conversational warmth.

### 79. Nor

**Source: PDF p. 51.** After a negative, coordinate appropriately: “cannot eat nor sleep” → “cannot eat or sleep”; or “can neither eat nor sleep”; or “cannot eat nor can he sleep.” Preserve the sentence's negative scope.

### 80. Noun used as verb

**Source: PDF pp. 51–52.** Source suspects conversions and proposes gift→give; hosted a dinner→gave a dinner; was chaired by→was chair of; headquarters in→has headquarters in; debuted→made its debut. **Adaptation:** these are historical stylistic objections; many ordinary/technical conversions are apt. Compare precision and naturalness, not parts-of-speech ancestry.

### 81. Offputting. Ongoing

**Source: PDF p. 52.** Source dislikes both as vague innovations: identify objectionable/disconcerting/distasteful if that is intended; omit *ongoing* where the program's continuation is already clear. **Adaptation:** both can convey useful current meaning; do not ban based on the book's verb-formation test.

### 82. One

**Source: PDF p. 52.** Keep impersonal-person reference consistent: “One must watch his step” → “one's step”; or use “You must watch your step.” Do not combine generic *one* with inconsistent person shifts.

### 83. One of the most

**Source: PDF p. 52.** Grammatically fine but threadbare as promotional introduction. State the actual distinctive property or use the ranking only when meaningful and supported.

### 84. -oriented

**Source: PDF p. 52.** Source finds it clumsy: manufacturing-oriented → chiefly concerned with manufacturing; situation-oriented skits → rely on situation. **Adaptation:** retain useful technical compounds (e.g., object-oriented); do not lengthen a clear term merely to evade the suffix.

### 85. Partially

**Source: PDF pp. 52–53.** Source prefers *partially* for degree of a state, *partly* for physical part/whole: partially resigned; log partly submerged; partly in and out / part in, part out. **Adaptation:** useful distinction when needed, not mandatory churn where overlap is harmless.

### 86. Participle for verbal noun

**Source: PDF p. 53.** Book favors possessive before a gerund when the action is the subject of concern: “prospect of the Senate accepting” → “Senate's accepting.” But recast awkward constructions: “reconsideration of the whole matter's becoming necessary” → “if it becomes necessary to reconsider”; dissatisfaction with decision being favorable → dissatisfaction with arbitrators' decision in the company's favor. **Adaptation:** preserve natural constructions; do not force possessives at the expense of clarity.

### 87. People

**Source: PDF p. 53.** Book distinguishes political *the people* from audience/customer *the public*, and prefers *persons* after numbers. **Adaptation:** do not ban “six people”; retain useful political/public distinctions only when real.

### 88. Personalize

**Source: PDF p. 53.** Source objects to showy instruction: “highly personalized affair” → “highly personal”; “personalize stationery” → “design a letterhead.” **Adaptation:** *personalized* and *personal* can differ; preserve customization when intended, and do not invent the particular customization.

### 89. Personally

**Source: PDF pp. 53–54.** “Personally, I thought…” → “I thought…” unless contrasting one's own view with an institutional/other view.

### 90. Possess

**Source: PDF p. 54.** Do not substitute impressive wording for *have/own*: “possessed courage” → “had courage/was brave”; “fortunate possessor of” → “lucky enough to own.” Source explicitly says the word is not incorrect. Preserve technical meanings.

### 91. Presently

**Source: PDF p. 54.** Can mean soon or now; source recommends reserving it for soon. Prefer explicit *soon/now* if time is material and ambiguous.

### 92. Prestigious

**Source: PDF p. 54.** A last-resort praise word. Name relevant standing, recognition, or accomplishment when known; omit unsupported evaluation.

## R through S

### 93. Refer

**Source: PDF p. 54.** Cross-reference to **Allude**; preserve the lookup key.

### 94. Regretful

**Source: PDF p. 54.** The person feels regret; an unfortunate event is *regrettable*: “regretful breakdown” → “regrettable breakdown.”

### 95. Relate

**Source: PDF p. 54.** Book dislikes rapport sense: “I relate well to Janet” → “we see things similarly/have much in common.” **Adaptation:** those alternatives are not always equivalent; preserve intended empathy/connection and don't enforce the historical ban.

### 96. Respective. Respectively

**Source: PDF p. 54.** Cut when mapping is already clear: fiction under respective authors → under authors. “Jones and Cummings won the mile and two-mile respectively” can become “Jones won the mile; Cummings, the two-mile.” **Adaptation:** retain *respectively* if it efficiently preserves a nontrivial ordered mapping.

### 97. Secondly, thirdly, etc

**Source: PDF p. 54.** Source prefers *first, second, third*. Treat as consistency/economy, not a claim that *firstly* is impossible.

### 98. Shall. Will

**Source: PDF pp. 54–55.** Source gives historical first-person *shall* for futurity, *will* for determination, reversing roles in second/third persons, with drowning examples. **Adaptation:** do not impose this obsolete default; preserve future, intention, promise, and any domain-defined legal/requirements modality. Never swap *shall/must/may/will* mechanically.

### 99. So

**Source: PDF p. 55.** Source dislikes empty intensifying *so good/warm/delightful*. Keep degree/result constructions and intentional conversational emphasis.

### 100. Sort of

**Source: PDF p. 55.** Cross-reference to **Kind of**; retain classification/approximation distinctions.

### 101. Split infinitive

**Source: PDF p. 55; V.14 p. 70.** IV discourages it except for stress; V explicitly allows it for naturalness and precision: “to really like” can be better than stiffer alternatives. Do not create ambiguity or stiffness to unsplit an infinitive.

### 102. State

**Source: PDF p. 55.** Avoid as inflated substitute for *say/remark*; apt when expressing something explicitly/fully: “state objections.” Preserve technical/documentary *state* where useful.

### 103. Student body

**Source: PDF p. 55.** Usually *students*: “member of the student body” → “student”; “popular with the student body” → “liked by students.” Keep institutional collective meaning when it matters.

## T through W

### 104. Than

**Source: PDF p. 55.** Supply missing comparison terms: closer to mother than father → either “than **to** father” or “than father **is**.” Cormorant/heron example repeats *like* to show the compared resemblances. Ask/flag if the intended comparison cannot be inferred.

### 105. Thanking you in advance

**Source: PDF p. 56.** Source regards it as presumptuous; make a courteous request and acknowledge later. **Adaptation:** tone/context judgment, not a universal prohibition on a conventional polite phrase.

### 106. That. Which

**Source: PDF p. 56.** Book prefers *that* for restrictive identification, *which* plus commas for supplementary information: mower **that is broken** identifies one; mower, **which is broken,** adds a fact. It acknowledges restrictive *which* occurs and sometimes suits the sentence. **Adaptation:** preserve scope and punctuation; do not claim restrictive *which* is ungrammatical, especially across dialects/house styles.

### 107. The foreseeable future

**Source: PDF p. 56.** Vague time horizon. Specify the relevant period if evidence permits; otherwise preserve and flag the uncertainty rather than fabricate a date.

### 108. The truth is. . . . The fact is. . . 

**Source: PDF p. 56.** Usually delete the announcement and state the supported proposition. Keep intentional contrast/discourse effects where needed.

### 109. They. He or She

**Source: PDF pp. 56–57.** Book rejects singular *they*, discusses generic *he*, and offers pluralization, pronoun omission, or second person to avoid repetitive *he or she*: writer/his readers → writers/their readers; writer/readers; you/your readers. **Adaptation:** preserve singular *they* and people's pronouns; do not import generic *he* as default. Other recasts remain useful, but changing number/person can alter scope or voice.

### 110. This

**Source: PDF p. 57.** Supply a clarifying noun when an entire preceding sentence makes the referent vague. Source “This is the first visible evidence…” after a groundbreaking becomes “The ceremony afforded…” **Adaptation:** only identify a referent that the source supports; do not overcorrect perfectly clear *this*.

### 111. Thrust

**Source: PDF pp. 57–58.** Vague managerial force metaphor often → *point/purpose*. “tremendous thrust” needs content; piston thrust is literal/technical. “thrust of his letter” → “point he made.”

### 112. Tortuous. Torturous

**Source: PDF p. 58.** Winding/convoluted versus painful: a tortuous road; a torturous ordeal.

### 113. Transpire

**Source: PDF p. 58.** Book favors “become known” over “happen.” **Adaptation:** choose *happen/emerge/become known* to clarify the intended distinction, rather than enforcing etymology.

### 114. Try

**Source: PDF p. 58.** Source prefers *try to* in formal prose but explicitly accepts conversational *try and*. Preserve register and meaning.

### 115. Type

**Source: PDF p. 58.** Repair telegraphic compounds where unclear: “that type employee” → “that kind of employee”; “type publicity” → “kind of publicity”; “home-type hotels” → “homelike hotels”; “new type plane” → “plane of a new design.” **Adaptation:** preserve technical category/type distinctions.

### 116. Unique

**Source: PDF p. 58.** Source uses the absolute sense “without equal”: *most/very unique* → *unique*; select an actual degree word if meaning “unusual.” Preserve whether the claim is uniqueness or rarity; do not invent either.

### 117. Utilize

**Source: PDF p. 59.** Prefer *use* when equivalent: utilized dishwasher → used dishwasher. “utilized facilities” → “used toilet” is source's de-euphemizing example, **not permission to infer a toilet** from unspecified facilities. Keep a genuinely distinct technical usage.

### 118. Verbal

**Source: PDF p. 59.** *Verbal* concerns words and can include writing; *oral* specifically concerns speech. “oral agreement” is clearer where spoken versus written matters. Source's “word for word” wording should not be extended into a claim that *verbal* always means *verbatim*.

### 119. Very

**Source: PDF p. 59.** Use sparingly; choose a stronger accurate word when genuine emphasis is needed. Do not exaggerate by replacing *very tired* with an unsupported extreme.

### 120. While

**Source: PDF pp. 59–60.** Identify the intended relation: simultaneity, contrast, or concession. Source permits concessive *while* if unambiguous (“While I admire his energy…”), suggests semicolon for mere coordination, and warns that hot days/while cold nights can misleadingly imply simultaneity. **Adaptation:** not a literal-time-only ban; choose *although/whereas/but* if they prevent ambiguity.

### 121. -wise

**Source: PDF p. 60.** Avoid catchall compounds like *taxwise/pricewise/marriagewise* where ordinary phrasing is clearer; retain established directional/manner forms such as *clockwise* and genuine terms.

### 122. Worth while

**Source: PDF p. 60.** Replace vague approval with the action/value: books not worth while → not worth reading; worthwhile project → promising/useful/valuable/exciting **if supported**. Do not manufacture positive qualities.

### 123. Would

**Source: PDF p. 60.** Habitual *would* may be redundant with “once a year/every day”: once a year he would visit → he visited. Preserve habit if removing it changes the sense. Mark the transition to a particular event: after habitual breakfast routine, add “One morning in January…” before the footprints episode, **only if such temporal specificity is supplied**; otherwise use a supported transition such as “On one occasion.”
