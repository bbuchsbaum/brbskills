## R01. Singular possessives

*Source: PDF p. 11.*

Add **'s** to a singular noun, including one ending in *s*: *Charles's friend; Burns's poems; the witch's malice*. The book excepts certain ancient names ending in *-es* or *-is*, *Jesus'*, and conventional *for conscience' sake / for righteousness' sake*. An *of* construction often reads better for these names: *the laws of Moses; the temple of Isis*.

Possessive pronouns have no apostrophe: *hers, its, theirs, yours, ours*. Indefinite pronouns do: *one's rights; somebody else's umbrella*. Distinguish *it's* (“it is”) from possessive *its*: *It's a wise dog that scratches its own fleas.*

**Editorial adaptation:** Honor the user's or publisher's consistent possessive convention for proper names. Do not describe an alternative house style as ungrammatical. The PDF's *ones rights* is an apparent source typo; do not copy it.

## R02. The serial comma

*Source: PDF pp. 11–12.*

In a series of three or more items joined by a single conjunction, put a comma after each item except the last: *red, white, and blue; gold, silver, or copper; opened the letter, read it, and made a note*. Apply the same pattern to phrases and clauses. Preserve an organization's official spelling, as in *Little, Brown and Company*.

**Editorial adaptation:** Prefer consistency and disambiguation over a universal comma mandate when a venue specifies another style.

## R03. Parenthetic and restrictive material

*Source: PDF pp. 12–14.*

Set off an interruption with **both** commas, or with neither when the interruption is slight. Never leave one half of a pair. *My brother you will be pleased to hear, is well* → *My brother, you will be pleased to hear, is well*. *Marjorie's husband, Colonel Nelson paid us a visit* requires either a closing comma or a genuinely restrictive construction.

Use commas around direct address: *Well, Susan, this is a mess*. Treat a following degree or supplementary title as parenthetic: *Horace Fulsome, Ph.D., presided*. The book likewise treats *etc., i.e., e.g.* as parenthetic when inserted. Do not insert commas into restrictive identifiers: *the novelist Jane Austen; William the Conqueror; Billy the Kid*. It favors *James Wright Jr.* without a comma because *Jr.* identifies which person.

Distinguish **supplement** from **identification**. *The audience, which was initially indifferent, became interested* adds information about an already identified audience. *People who live in glass houses...* selects a class of people; no commas. The same distinction governs *when/where* clauses, participles, and appositives:

| Restrictive: identifies which | Nonrestrictive: supplies an aside |
| --- | --- |
| *People sitting in the rear couldn't hear.* | *Uncle Bert, being slightly deaf, moved forward.* |
| *My cousin Bob is a harpist.* | *Our oldest daughter, Mary, sings.* |

Date patterns in the book: *April 6, 1986; Wednesday, November 14, 1990; February to July, 1992*. The day–month form takes no internal comma: *6 April 1988*. Separate a preceding subordinate clause or substantial introductory phrase from the main clause: *Partly by fighting, partly by diplomacy, they expanded eastward*.

**Editorial adaptation:** Commas can change which people or things a statement covers. Preserve that scope. Honor present venue conventions for dates, degrees, titles, and brief introductory phrases; the book's formatting preferences are not all compulsory grammar.

## R04. Coordinating independent clauses

*Source: PDF p. 14.*

Use a comma before a conjunction joining independent clauses: *The records disappeared, and the early history cannot be reconstructed*; *The situation is perilous, but escape is still possible*. The book extends this guidance to causal *as/for*, *or/nor*, and concurrent *while*.

If an introductory subordinate clause begins the second member, do not automatically insert another comma immediately after the conjunction: *The situation is perilous, but if we act promptly, escape remains possible*. With a shared subject, omit the comma before a closely connected *and*: *He has experience and is competent*. The book finds a comma useful before a contrasting *but*: *I have heard the arguments, but am unconvinced*.

**Editorial adaptation:** Choose punctuation to clarify the actual structure and rhythm; modern editorial practice often omits the comma between short compound predicates, including those joined by *but*.

## R05. Independent clauses without a conjunction

*Source: PDF pp. 14–15.*

Use a semicolon for closely related independent clauses, or make two sentences. Add a coordinating conjunction if using a comma. These alternatives communicate different degrees and kinds of connection:

- *It is nearly half past five; we cannot reach town before dark.*
- *It is nearly half past five. We cannot reach town before dark.*
- *It is nearly half past five, and we cannot reach town before dark.*

An adverb such as *therefore, thus, accordingly, besides,* or *then* is not a coordinating conjunction: *I had never been there; besides, it was dark*. The book expressly allows a comma between very short parallel clauses and in easy conversation: *Man proposes, God disposes*; *The gates swung apart, the bridge fell*; *I hardly knew him, he was so changed*.

**Editorial adaptation:** Repair accidental comma splices, but preserve deliberate parataxis when it produces a clear rhetorical effect.

## R06. Sentences and purposeful fragments

*Source: PDF pp. 15–16.*

Do not substitute periods for commas unintentionally: *I met them on a liner. Coming home from Liverpool* → join the participial phrase to the sentence. Likewise, *She was an interesting talker. A woman who had travelled widely* normally needs joining.

An emphatic fragment can work: *Again and again he called out. No reply.* Fragments also belong naturally in dialogue. The emphasis must repay the break; otherwise it reads as an error.

## R07. Colons

*Source: PDF pp. 16–17.*

Follow a complete clause with a colon to introduce particulars, an appositive, amplification, or supporting quotation. Do not separate a verb from its complement or a preposition from its object:

- *A whittler requires: a knife, wood, and a porch* → *A whittler requires three props: a knife, wood, and a porch*.
- *Knowledge grows from: theory, practice, and error* → remove the colon.

A second complete clause can explain the first: *Animal burial had a directness about it: there was no stop at the undertaker's*. A colon can introduce a quotation supporting the preceding assertion. Conventional uses include formal-letter salutations, clock time, title/subtitle divisions, and chapter/verse: *Dear Mr. Montague:; 10:48; Practical Calligraphy: An Introduction; Nehemiah 11:7*.

## R08. Dashes

*Source: PDF p. 17.*

Use a dash for an abrupt break, interruption, long appositive, or summary. It separates more strongly than a comma, less formally than a colon, and less quietly than parentheses:

- Interruption: *His first thought—if he had any thought—was to return to bed*.
- Appositive: *The axle made a noise—a grinding, chattering rasp*.
- Summary: *The late sunrise, sharp breeze, falling leaves—all announced winter*.

When ordinary punctuation works, prefer it. *Violence—the kind on television—is not honestly violent—there lies its harm* → *Violence, the kind on television, is not honestly violent. There lies its harm.* The example illustrates stronger sentence architecture, not a ban on dashes.

## R09. Agreement

*Source: PDF pp. 17–19.*

Make the verb agree with the grammatical subject, not an intervening noun: *The flavor of youth—its trials and joys—is remembered*. In *one of the scientists who have tackled the problem*, *who* refers to plural *scientists*. Use singular verbs with *each, either, everyone, everybody, neither, nobody,* and *someone*.

The book permits singular or plural *none* according to meaning: singular for “not one,” plural when several people or things are conceived together. *None of us is perfect* and *None are so fallible as the certain* illustrate its distinction.

Subjects joined by *and* normally take a plural verb: *The walrus and carpenter were walking*. Treat inseparable units as singular: *bread and butter was all she served; give and take is essential; the long and short of it is...*. *Each/every* can make a coordinated expression singular: *Every window, picture, and mirror was smashed*.

Additions introduced by *with, as well as, in addition to, except, together with,* or *no less than* do not change a singular subject's number: *His speech as well as his manner is objectionable*. A linking verb agrees with the subject, not a plural complement: *The trouble with truth is its many varieties*.

Some apparent plurals are idiomatic singulars: *Politics is...; headquarters is...*; others remain plural: *quarters are...*. The book distinguishes a book's *contents* from the contents of a jar according to what is meant.

**Editorial adaptation:** Treat collective nouns, *none*, and plural-looking nouns according to meaning, English variety, and established usage. Do not blindly prescribe singular *contents* for every reference to a book. A grammatically singular *everyone* does not require a gendered pronoun.

## R10. Pronoun case

*Source: PDF pp. 19–21.*

Choose case by grammatical function. In *Give the work to whoever looks idle*, *whoever* is the subject of *looks*; the object of *to* is the whole clause. Test embedded clauses without the parenthetical words:

- *the candidate whom we think will win* → *the candidate who we think will win* (*he will win*).
- *the candidate who we hope to elect* → *the candidate whom we hope to elect* (*elect him*).

Supply an understood verb where omission leaves a comparison ambiguous: *Horace admires Jessica more than I* → *...more than I do*; *Polly loves cake more than me* → *...more than she loves me* if that is the intended comparison.

Use object pronouns as objects: *offered Shirley and him advice; meet the Baldwins and us; between you and me*. A coordinated subject takes ordinary subject pronouns: *Blake and myself stayed* → *Blake and I stayed*; *Howard and yourself brought lunch* → *Howard and you brought lunch*. Distinguish adjectival and noun-form possessives: *your glove; a glove of yours; one of mine*.

The book prefers possessive subjects for gerunds: *Mother objected to our driving*. A participle after a perception verb takes an object: *They heard him singing*. It distinguishes objection to *me asking* from objection to *my asking*: the person versus the act can be foregrounded differently. It also notes that grammatically possible *a group of us taxpayers* is awkward; recasting may improve it, though the result may not be exactly equivalent.

**Editorial adaptation:** Avoid hypercorrect or stiff constructions in ordinary prose. Standard modern usage allows more flexibility with predicative pronouns and subjects of *-ing* clauses than the book's formal defaults. Resolve real ambiguity rather than forcing *It was he* or *than I* into natural dialogue.

## R11. Dangling introductory modifiers

*Source: PDF p. 21.*

An opening participial phrase must refer to the grammatical subject: *Walking down the road, he saw a woman* makes **him** the walker. If she was walking, put the phrase beside *woman* or recast. Apply the same attachment test to introductory adjectives, appositives, and phrases preceded by prepositions or conjunctions:

| Misattached | Repaired |
| --- | --- |
| *On arriving in Chicago, his friends met him.* | *On arriving in Chicago, he was met by his friends.* |
| *A soldier of valor, they entrusted him with the city.* | *A soldier of valor, he was entrusted with the city.* |
| *Young and inexperienced, the task seemed easy to me.* | *Young and inexperienced, I thought the task easy.* |
| *Without a friend to counsel him, the temptation proved irresistible.* | *Without a friend to counsel him, he found the temptation irresistible.* |

The house, not the buyer, is dilapidated; the person, not the clock, is wondering what to do. Notice that passive voice can be the right repair.

## Compact glossary

*Source: PDF pp. 76–80.*

Use these distinctions to diagnose an actual editorial decision; do not turn grammar terminology into extra explanation for the user.

| Distinction | Practical diagnostic |
| --- | --- |
| Subject / predicate / complement / object | Who or what is the clause about? What does the verb assert? What completes it? What receives the action? Use these functions to check agreement, case, and attachment. |
| Phrase / clause / independent / dependent | A clause contains a subject–predicate relationship; an independent clause can stand alone. A phrase lacks that complete relationship. Choose sentence punctuation accordingly. |
| Restrictive / nonrestrictive modifier | Does this identify which referent, or add a removable supplement about an already identified referent? Commas can change population scope. |
| Antecedent / relative / appositive | Locate what a pronoun or relative refers to; an appositive renames or identifies a noun. Keep the connection unambiguous. |
| Coordinating / correlative conjunction | Join elements of equivalent function; align the two arms of *both–and, either–or,* and related constructions. |
| Transitive / intransitive / linking verb | A transitive verb takes an object; an intransitive one need not; a linking verb connects the subject with a complement. Some verbs have several uses. |
| Active / passive voice | *Janet played the guitar* vs *The guitar was played by Janet*. Do not confuse weak diction, intransitivity, or every use of *be* with passive voice. |
| Gerund / participle / infinitive | An *-ing* noun, a verbal adjective, and a *to* construction serve different grammatical jobs. Diagnose the job, not the surface ending. |
| Auxiliary / modal | Auxiliaries carry tense and voice; modals can carry necessity, obligation, permission, probability, possibility, ability, or tentativeness. Removing them can change the claim. |
| Loose / periodic sentence | A loose sentence gives the main assertion first and appends material; a periodic sentence delays its completion. Both are useful when suited to the thought. |
| Tense / chronology | Verb forms position actions in time; a coherent narration still needs changes of tense when times differ. |
| Transition / coherence | A connector should express the actual relationship between ideas. Inserting *moreover* cannot create a missing logical relation. |
